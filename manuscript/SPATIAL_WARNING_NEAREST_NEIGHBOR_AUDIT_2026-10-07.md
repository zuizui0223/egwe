# Spatial movement warning -> future demography: nearest-neighbour literature audit

Date: 2026-10-07

Status: **bounded literature audit; not a systematic review and not evidence that no such study can exist.**

## Question

After Love & Otto (2026), what is the narrowest defensible empirical gap?

The target is not simply "early warning in wild populations" and not simply "movement relates to demography".

The target is:

> **A population-level spatial organization metric computed from wild telemetry at time t is frozen before outcome evaluation and tested for incremental out-of-sample prediction of a later population-level demographic endpoint, beyond current demographic state and contemporaneous spatial scale.**

This audit searches the nearest conceptual and empirical neighbours and records why each does or does not close that target.

## 1. Love & Otto (2026) — spatial-state detection, not future demography

Love, N.E. & Otto, S.P. (2026). *Spatial signatures of group cohesion*. Methods in Ecology and Evolution 17:2755–2769. doi:10.1111/2041-210x.70375.

Contribution:
- introduces scale-free CV metrics of inter-individual distances;
- simulations quantify ability to detect elongation and splitting;
- Bathurst caribou example detects seasonal rut-associated splitting and later re-cohesion;
- jackdaw example separates mobbing and transiting spatial configurations.

Boundary:
- the forecast target is spatial fragmentation/cohesion itself;
- no later population-level recruitment, survival, abundance decline or extinction endpoint is evaluated;
- no held-out future-demography comparison against current abundance/range state is performed.

Thus Love & Otto provide a strong spatial **state indicator** and explicitly motivate early warning, but not a validated population-demographic forecast.

## 2. Cerini, Childs & Clements (2023) — the exact conceptual precursor, but no original validation

Cerini, F., Childs, D.Z. & Clements, C.F. (2023). *A predictive timeline of wildlife population collapse*. Nature Ecology & Evolution 7:320–331. doi:10.1038/s41559-023-01985-2.

This Perspective proposes a sequential collapse timeline:

1. behaviour changes first;
2. fitness-related morphology changes;
3. demographic rates such as births change;
4. abundance declines last.

This is highly relevant because it explicitly argues that behavioural information should forecast decline earlier than abundance alone.

However:
- the paper is a Perspective;
- its data-availability statement says no original data were used;
- it proposes a predictive framework rather than validating a wild telemetry-to-future-demography forecast.

It therefore sharpens the hypothesis rather than closing it.

## 3. Cerini et al. (2025) — empirical validation, but in a laboratory microcosm

Cerini, F. et al. (2025). *Multivariate signals of population collapse in a high-throughput ecological experiment*. Ecology. doi:10.1002/ecy.70197.

This is the strongest empirical support for the 2023 timeline.

In autonomously monitored Paramecium populations:
- under gradually increasing pollutant stress, movement-speed changes occurred before morphology changes and before abundance decline;
- behavioural signals appeared roughly two generations before abundance decline and one generation before abundance-based EWS under the GAM analysis;
- the sequence did not reproduce under the predator press disturbance.

This establishes that behavioural change can carry earlier decline information in a controlled population-collapse experiment.

It does **not** close the present target because:
- it is a laboratory microcosm;
- the behavioural variable is individual movement speed, not population spatial fragmentation/cohesion;
- it is not a wild population-year telemetry forecast;
- disturbance dependence already shows that portability cannot be assumed.

Therefore the field gap is narrower than "behaviour before collapse has never been validated".

## 4. Hefley, Tyre & Blankenship (2013) — wild extinction warning exists, but from abundance time series

Hefley, T.J., Tyre, A.J. & Blankenship, E.E. (2013). *Statistical indicators and state-space population models predict extinction in a population of bobwhite quail*. Theoretical Ecology 6:319–331. doi:10.1007/s12080-013-0195-3.

This paper prevents an overstatement that predictive early warning has never been attempted in wild populations.

Using two independent long-term field count series, the authors:
- fit state-space population-growth models;
- derived a system-specific statistical indicator for a transcritical extinction threshold;
- estimated threshold-crossing times, including future crossing estimates.

Important boundary:
- the predictors are abundance/population-growth time series;
- the target is an inferred dynamical threshold, not a held-out later telemetry-demography outcome;
- no animal-movement or population-cohesion metric is involved;
- the future threshold estimates were model projections, not a rolling held-out validation of observed later demographic outcomes.

Thus wild-population early-warning modelling is not new; movement-derived incremental forecasting remains distinct.

## 5. Paun et al. (2022) — movement explicitly called a precursor, but future decline is not tested

Paun, I. et al. (2022). *Inferring spatially varying animal movement characteristics using a hierarchical continuous-time velocity model*. Ecology Letters 25:2726–2738. doi:10.1111/ele.14117.

The paper explicitly states that altered movement and migration can be early warning signs and precursors to population declines.

Empirical work:
- six years of Serengeti wildebeest telemetry;
- spatially varying movement characteristics;
- environmental effects on movement and migration pathways.

Boundary:
- the empirical endpoint is movement structure itself;
- later population-level decline is not the predictive response;
- no prospective incremental demographic forecast is evaluated.

This is another case in which the early-warning interpretation outruns direct demographic validation.

## 6. Poulton et al. (2024) — telemetry and survival are integrated, but the estimand is individual spatial hazard

Poulton, A.J. et al. (2024). *Bayesian estimation of spatially varying mortality risk using tagged animal data*. Methods in Ecology and Evolution 15:2101–2117. doi:10.1111/2041-210X.14428.

This is an important counter-neighbour because telemetry and demography are jointly modelled.

The method:
- combines movement paths with time-to-event survival data;
- estimates spatially varying mortality hazard;
- is demonstrated on 147 caribou and 390 Atlantic cod.

Why it does not close the target:
- the prediction object is individual mortality hazard conditional on where animals move;
- it does not ask whether a population-level cohesion/fragmentation statistic at time t adds prediction for later population-level demography;
- there is no Love–Otto-type spatial state compressed at t and evaluated as an early-warning coordinate against a future population outcome.

The distinction is between **movement-conditioned hazard estimation** and **movement-state early warning of population trajectory**.

## 7. Sigaud et al. (2017) — movement can causally contribute to decline, but this is retrospective mechanism, not warning validation

Sigaud, M. et al. (2017). *Collective decision-making promotes fitness loss in a fusion-fission society*. Ecology Letters 20:33–40. doi:10.1111/ele.12698.

In wild bison:
- collective movement/social information increased use of agricultural ecological traps;
- adult mortality increased by about 12% through hunting;
- the population declined by approximately 50% over less than a decade.

This is strong evidence that collective movement can contribute mechanistically to demography.

Boundary:
- the study reconstructs a movement -> habitat choice -> mortality -> population decline mechanism;
- it does not freeze an earlier spatial-cohesion indicator and prospectively score later population decline;
- it does not test incremental predictive information beyond current demographic state.

This is causal/retrospective linkage, not an early-warning validation.

## 8. Benson et al. (2025) — very broad wild cohesion data, but drivers rather than future demography

Benson, J.F. et al. (2025). *Intrinsic and environmental drivers of pairwise cohesion in wild Canis social groups*. Ecology 106:e4492. doi:10.1002/ecy.4492.

Strength:
- GPS telemetry from 574 individuals across six Canis species, 74 study areas and 15 countries;
- directly quantifies pairwise cohesion from near-simultaneous positions;
- many systems also have group-size and breeding-status information.

Boundary:
- models intrinsic/environmental drivers of cohesion;
- does not use cohesion as a frozen predictor of later population-level growth/recruitment/extinction.

This is a potentially valuable future validation resource, but not an existing closure of the gap.

## 9. Morales et al. (2010) — the bridge has been recognized for >15 years

Morales, J.M. et al. (2010). *Building the bridge between animal movement and population dynamics*. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 365:2289–2301. doi:10.1098/rstb.2010.0082.

The review explicitly states that although mechanistic links between movement and population dynamics are intuitive, explicit connection is rarely attained and asks when movement knowledge is necessary for predicting population dynamics.

This is important historical context: the present gap is not newly invented by Love & Otto. Love & Otto make one particular population-level spatial state measurable; the longstanding unresolved issue is whether such movement information adds predictive value for population dynamics.

## 10. Oliver et al. (2026) — current field-level call confirms that predictive validation remains unfinished

Oliver, R.Y. et al. (2026). *A call to integrate animal movement into biodiversity indicators*. Nature Reviews Biodiversity 2:467–479. doi:10.1038/s44358-026-00173-x.

The Perspective argues that current biodiversity indicators are often too slow and insufficiently explanatory or predictive, and that movement data can add:
- biological realism;
- mechanistic understanding;
- early-warning capacity.

It recommends:
- grounding connectivity metrics in empirical movement;
- developing indicators that flag rapid change;
- investing in modelling and attribution studies connecting movement to biodiversity loss and recovery.

This is strong evidence that the movement-to-predictive-indicator bridge is still considered an active research need in 2026.

## 11. Russo et al. (2026) — movement as biodiversity indicator, not a validated fate forecast

Russo et al. / Blakey, Niesner, Abelson et al. (2026). *Monitoring animal movement diversity as a component of biodiversity*. Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment. doi:10.1002/fee.70038.

This framework treats movement diversity itself as an under-measured biodiversity dimension and organizes movement metrics across levels.

It supports the monitoring relevance of movement but does not test a fixed spatial movement score against later population-level demography.

## What is already known and must not be claimed as novel

The current project must **not** claim novelty for any of the following:

- early-warning analysis in wild populations;
- behavioural change preceding abundance decline in general;
- movement being related to survival or reproduction;
- movement contributing causally to population decline;
- GPS-derived cohesion metrics;
- telemetry-demography joint models;
- the idea that movement should enter biodiversity indicators.

Each has clear prior literature.

## Narrow remaining gap after the audit

The strongest defensible gap is:

> **We found no close precedent in this bounded audit that takes a population-level spatial organization/cohesion statistic from wild telemetry at time t, freezes it before outcome evaluation, and tests whether it adds out-of-sample prediction of a later population-level demographic endpoint beyond current demographic state and contemporaneous spatial scale.**

This is deliberately narrower than saying no wild early-warning study exists.

Four ingredients must occur together:

1. **wild population telemetry** rather than laboratory behaviour;
2. **population-level spatial organization** rather than individual speed/hazard;
3. **later population-level demography** rather than current movement state or individual survival hazard;
4. **future-held-out incremental validation** against a contemporaneous demographic/spatial baseline.

The nearest neighbours generally satisfy one to three of these, not all four.

## Why the Ya Ha Tinda test is appropriately targeted

The frozen Ya Ha Tinda protocol asks exactly this narrow question:

[
M_0: R_{next} sim R_{previous} + log S_t
]

versus

[
M_1: R_{next} sim R_{previous} + log S_t + CVratio_t.
]

where:
- `CVratio_t` is the pinned Love–Otto population spatial metric in autumn;
- `S_t` is contemporaneous spatial scale;
- `R_previous` is the already-observed calf:cow state;
- `R_next` is the later calf:cow state;
- scoring is rolling-origin, one-step-ahead.

If runnable, this is therefore not "another movement paper". It directly tests whether the spatial-state metric earns the stronger word **warning**.

## Current execution boundary

The Ya Ha Tinda raw GPS archive remains behind a Movebank login/license/permission interface in the current execution environment.

A teaching-repository mirror was audited and rejected:
- 138,433 fixes;
- 31 GPS individuals;
- years 2001–2005 only;
- generated from an older Elk_data.csv subset;
- timezone-naive timestamps.

It is not the frozen 175-animal, 2001–2020 archive and will not be used.

The prospective-style analysis contract therefore remains valid and closed pending official source bytes. No sampling threshold, season or outcome is relaxed to make the test runnable.

## Search ceiling

This audit is bounded rather than exhaustive. It used targeted searches around:
- wildlife early-warning / extinction prediction;
- animal movement as precursor;
- telemetry + survival/demography;
- group cohesion / collective movement;
- movement indicators and biodiversity monitoring.

A future systematic review could identify additional cases. Until then, manuscript wording should use **"we found no close precedent in our targeted audit"**, not **"no study has ever tested"**.
